# Content strategy

1. Locate the reader's situation in businessContext.icp and the actual scope of businessContext.offer. Identify one decision the reader faces and their likely objection. Use strategy.voice and brandKit for identity; supplied evidence establishes facts. Complete this step when the angle names a situation and a useful outcome of reading, rather than a broad topic.
2. Choose the editorial form the material supports: an explained distinction, worked diagnostic, tradeoff, sourced observation or verified experience. A customer story needs an actual supplied account; a resource offer needs an available resource. Format mix is a tie-breaker between equally useful treatments. Record the decision and form in the brief's angle and constraints, using the existing output schema.
   An idea or source may enumerate a whole lifecycle. Select the single decision worth explaining in this post rather than turning every category into a slide. The idea is research input, not a mandatory table of contents. Give the angle enough space to state the demonstration completely within its field limit.
3. Outline the reasoning before the hook. For a diagnostic, show the input, an observable result, its interpretation and the next action. Include the discriminating observation that justifies the proposed check over a plausible alternative. Build this observation from the current idea and evidence; an ambiguous symptom cannot establish a cause. If the cause remains uncertain, demonstrate the next observation that would distinguish the alternatives rather than assert a diagnosis. For a tradeoff, explain when each choice fits and its cost. For an observation, connect the supplied fact to a bounded implication. Complete this step when a reader can apply the explanation without requesting a missing guide.
   Preserve the relationship between requirements when adapting a source: two conditions that both apply must not become alternatives, and approval does not waive an eligibility criterion without an explicit exception. When the source describes a required control and the post proposes an additional planning artifact or workflow, distinguish that proposal from the actual requirement. Do not introduce control/readiness/planning terminology when the topic has no such distinction. Express the reader-facing implication in concrete language; do not copy this editorial instruction into the brief or public copy.
4. Develop the chosen editorial form, then an opening that accurately introduces it. An explained distinction can use a comparison or decision diagram without a fictional actor. Add a worked example only when its concrete inputs reveal something beyond a paraphrase of the rule; naming a person and restating both outcomes adds no value. If the reasoning has alternative outcomes, show separate conditional branches, never a chronology in which the same unchanged case has both incompatible outcomes. Use supplied facts or explicitly labelled fictional inputs only when that chosen example requires them. For a worked example, instantiate the values needed to follow the reasoning: a named task plus an instruction to compare unspecified criteria is not yet a demonstration. Show the relevant choices, what the test should reveal and how that result changes the next action. Do not invent a measured outcome or add decorative detail. A source documenting one product event supports that event, not a general operational rule for every system. State that scope in the opening as well as the explanation. Keep empirical results and product capabilities within the evidence. A fictional example illustrates reasoning; it does not establish business impact.
5. End once the useful point is delivered. Use a CTA only when it follows naturally from that point and the requested action is possible. With a reader question as the ending, phrase prose diagnostic checks as statements explaining what to inspect and how to interpret it; do not add competing questions. Review recentBodies for a different problem, mechanism or takeaway. Repair the explanation itself when it is weak, preserving useful reasoning through factual corrections.

## Calibration

Insufficient: “Vérifiez le contexte, les droits, la source et la validation humaine” followed by four question slides. These are categories of work left to the reader, even if a fictional ticket is mentioned in the introduction.

Useful worked distinction: in an explicitly fictional case, a user can sign in but cannot open one folder. The proposed response cites a forgotten-password procedure. Show the exact mismatch, then propose checking whether the access issue affects one folder or all folders and finding the applicable access procedure. This demonstrates why a citation alone does not make an instruction relevant. It makes no claim about measured effectiveness or a real product. This is a calibration example, not a topic or template to repeat for other workspaces.

The brief stage returns a brief, the writer stage a draft. Preserve the surrounding role's schema, evidence rules and output limits.


## Make the example’s conditions carry its conclusion

Before finalizing a worked example, trace the actor, object, relevant condition and expected or observed outcome. State the condition that distinguishes the chosen outcome from the plausible alternative. Keep these identities and conditions consistent throughout; do not infer missing rights, membership or policy from the desired conclusion. For a fictional test, state what the test should show and what a contrary observation means, without claiming it was actually run. Supply only the detail needed to make the reasoning usable, and keep the illustrativeScenarios metadata synchronized with the public example.
